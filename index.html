02How to Play Chess — Rules for Beginners

Chess is a two-player strategy board game played on an 8×8 grid of 64 alternating light and dark squares. Each player begins with 16 pieces: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and eight pawns. The objective is simple: checkmate your opponent's king. That means placing the king under direct attack (called check) with no legal move that can remove the threat.

The Pieces and How They Move

PieceHow it moves
♔KingExactly one square in any direction — up, down, left, right, or diagonal. The king may never move into check.
♕QueenThe most powerful piece. Any number of squares in any direction — straight or diagonal — provided the path is clear.
♖RookAny number of squares horizontally or vertically. Rooks shine in the endgame when the board opens up.
♗BishopAny number of squares diagonally. Each bishop stays on the color of its starting square.
♘KnightAn L-shape: two squares in one direction and one square perpendicular. The only piece that can jump over others.
♙PawnForward one square (or two from its starting rank), captures diagonally. Has unique rules: en passant, promotion, and the two-square first move.

Special Rules Worth Knowing

Castling. A king and rook can move together as a single turn when neither has moved, the squares between them are empty, and the king is not in check or passing through check.

En passant. When an opponent's pawn moves two squares forward from its starting rank and lands beside your pawn, you may capture it as if it had moved only one square. This capture must be made immediately.

Pawn promotion. When a pawn reaches the opposite end of the board, it must be promoted to a queen, rook, bishop, or knight of the same color. Most players choose a queen in nearly every situation.

Check. When a king is under direct attack, the player must resolve the threat on their very next move.

Checkmate. When a king is in check and no legal move can remove the threat, the game ends and the attacking player wins.

Stalemate. When a player has no legal moves but their king is not in check, the game ends in a draw.

03Game Modes

RDJ Publishers Chess offers two distinct game modes, both running on the same engine and the same set of rules. Choose the mode that matches your mood and your company.

Two-Player Mode

Two-Player mode is designed for two people sharing a single device. Players take turns at the same screen, with the interface clearly indicating whose turn it is and which color holds which pieces. This mode is ideal for:

  • Teaching a child, sibling, or friend the rules in person.
  • Quick games during travel, breaks, or downtime.
  • Friendly matches without the pressure of an AI opponent.
  • Tactics practice with a sparring partner.

Move legally, undo freely, and restart whenever you want. There is no clock — play at whatever pace feels natural.

Play With Computer

In vs Computer mode, you always play as White and the AI plays as Black. This is the recommended mode for solo play and for players who want to practice against a consistent opponent. Three difficulty levels let you match the AI to your skill:

  • Easy — Casual play. The engine makes quicker, less optimal moves. Perfect for absolute beginners, kids, or anyone who just wants a relaxing game without pressure.
  • Medium — Balanced. The engine looks deeper and uses smarter heuristics, but still makes occasional mistakes. Most adults who know the rules will find this a satisfying challenge.
  • Hard — Deep search. The engine searches more plies and uses stronger evaluation. Expect long, deliberate play and tough tactics — ideal for intermediate players looking to be tested.

You can switch difficulty at any time before starting a new game.

05Chess Tips for Beginners

Whether you're playing your first game or your hundredth, a few principles will take you further than memorizing openings. Keep these in mind at the board.

Control the Center

The four central squares — e4, d4, e5, d5 — are the most important squares on the board. Pieces in the center control more squares and have more mobility than pieces stuck on the edge. As a beginner, develop your pieces toward the center before attacking.

Develop Your Pieces Early

In the opening, move each piece (knights and bishops first, then queen and rooks) off the back rank and into active squares. Avoid moving the same piece multiple times before you've developed the others.

Castle Early

Castling gets your king to safety and connects your rooks. Most experienced players castle within the first 8–10 moves. Leaving your king in the center for too long is one of the most common beginner mistakes.

Watch for Forks and Pins

A fork is when one piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at once — most commonly done with a knight. A pin is when a piece cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece (often the king) behind it. Learning to spot these tactics is the fastest way to win material.

Don't Hang Pieces

Hanging a piece means leaving it undefended where your opponent can capture it. Before every move, double-check: is the square I'm moving to safe? Is the square I'm moving from still defended?

Think Before You Move

Resist the urge to make moves quickly just because it's your turn. Look at every check, capture, and threat on the board first. Ask yourself: what does my opponent want? What is their best response to my move?
